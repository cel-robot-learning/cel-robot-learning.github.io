01

CEL: learning from a fleet’s own experience

Packing a phone box takes more than picking up a phone. The robot must place a manual, charging cable, charger, phone case and phone inside, then close the lid. An early placement error can make a later step harder. Human demonstrations and corrections help the robot learn this sequence, but supplying more corrections becomes harder as more robots practice at once. Meanwhile, those robots are generating real-world experience of their own. A successful attempt from one robot could contain useful actions for a situation where another struggled.

We developed Failure-guided Experience Retrieval Fine-Tuning (FERT) to turn this shared practice into learning. Successful attempts provide the actions to learn; failed attempts help identify which successes are worth revisiting. This is the core algorithm behind Collective Experience Learning (CEL). After human demonstrations and corrections establish a starting policy, FERT needs success-or-failure feedback, but no further expert demonstrations or corrective actions. Across five separately trained tasks, the resulting policies reach 93.3–99.2% success on robots outside the collection fleet. For phone packaging, training with FERT and 96 robots raises success from 79.2% to 98.3%.

02

From folding a T-shirt to packing a phone

We used five tasks to test how shared practice helps robots handle flexible objects, control contact and use tools. Each task has its own policy, trained on dual-arm AgiBot G2 robots. The robot packs a manual, charging cable, charger, phone case and phone, then aligns and closes the lid. The sequence takes roughly two to three minutes, and each placement matters: an item left in the wrong position can make a later step harder.

03

FERT: using failures to find what worked

Knowing that an attempt failed does not tell the robot which actions would have worked. In one phone-packaging example in our study, the left arm repeatedly adjusts the lid’s angle, yet the lid still topples. FERT uses observations from before a failed attempt ends to find similar moments in successful fleet executions, matching visual features and joint state over time. During training, it samples the successful actions around those matches more often. A failure from one robot can thus help select useful examples recorded by another. FERT continues from the policy established through human demonstrations and corrections. The updated policy returns to the fleet, and collection continues during optimization.

04

Getting the task right more often

We used the five tasks above to test whether this learning loop makes execution more reliable, including on robots outside the collection fleet. The largest gain comes in phone charging, where success rises from 60.0% to 97.5%. Across all five separately trained task policies, FERT raises mean success from 81.0% after human demonstrations and corrections to 97.3%, a gain of 16.3 percentage points. We tested each policy on two AgiBot G2 robots that had not collected training data, with 60 trials per robot and no human intervention. The gains therefore extend to other robots of the same platform. The charts below show success rates over these 120 trials per task, with 95% Wilson confidence intervals.

What changes when more robots contribute?

The reliability gains above come from shared practice. To see how performance changes as more robots contribute, we varied fleet size while keeping collection time per robot fixed. Larger fleets therefore provided more experience for FERT. On phone packaging, success reaches 50.8%, 86.7% and 98.3% with 4, 16 and 96 training robots, respectively. The 4-robot result falls below the starting policy’s 79.2%, while expanding from 16 to 96 robots adds 11.6 percentage points. Across the five tasks, FERT generally improves with larger fleets. One possible explanation for weaker small-fleet results is that their successful experience does not cover enough of the situations in which the policy fails. Choosing what to practice

A larger pool gives FERT more successful actions to learn from. Retrieval directs that learning toward the difficulties the policy is actually encountering. We tested this selection step by comparing full FERT with uniform sampling from the same success buffer on phone packaging with 16 training robots and 120 evaluation trials per model. Removing failure-guided retrieval lowers success from 86.7% to 64.2%, a drop of 22.5 percentage points. With the experience and other settings held fixed, this shows the benefit of choosing examples related to current failures.

FERT also learns from the movements around each matched segment, retaining one second before and after it. Removing this context lowers success from 86.7% to 57.5% in the same 16-robot phone-packaging experiment. The result suggests that these surrounding movements are an important part of the training example.

Keeping up with new experience

As the fleet keeps practicing, new failures change which examples deserve attention, and policy updates change the next attempts. We tested how long earlier retrieval priorities persist: retention factors of 0.80, 0.88 and 0.95 give 70.8%, 86.7% and 82.5% success. We also tested how often to send an updated policy back to the robots: every 100, 500 or 1,500 optimizer steps gives 75.0%, 86.7% or 80.0% success. The middle setting performs best in both tests, suggesting a balance between retaining earlier retrieval information and responding to new experience, and between policy refresh and further collection. These comparisons use phone packaging with 16 training robots and 120 evaluation trials per setting.

Reading the comparisons

FERT, RLT and RECAP start from the same selected multi-robot HG-DAgger policy for each task, then train separately. For phone charging, stool assembly and phone packaging, that starting policy uses initialization data pooled from HG-DAgger runs at different fleet sizes. The comparison includes two other ways to learn from experience: single-robot online reinforcement learning with RLT, and one round of offline reinforcement learning on fleet data with RECAP. Our implementations of both use human corrective actions during post-training; FERT does not. Collection scales and training settings differ between methods, so the plots compare the implementations reported in our study. For the human reference, six teleoperators each complete 20 trials per task.

05

Learning together without waiting for everyone

To keep FERT’s learning loop running, robots need to send recorded attempts to the training system and receive updated policies. Robots finish at different times, need resets and sometimes encounter faults. Waiting for every robot to finish a round would let one interruption hold up the others. We built the Fault-tolerant Asynchronous Training System (FATS), using RLinf, to sustain this cycle of practice and improvement. Robot interaction and policy inference run at the edge, while training runs in the cloud. HotSwarm manages workers as they leave, recover and rejoin, so available robots can keep collecting experience while training and policy updates continue.

When practice is interrupted

A 96-robot run shows how physical interruptions affect experience collection. Between approximately 10:30 and 18:30, 22 robots encountered at least one system error. Other robots continued operating, and some affected robots later resumed collection. Operators judged whether attempts succeeded and reset the environments, without providing corrective actions or additional demonstrations. The errors include joint, battery and sensor faults, as well as host-memory, network and startup failures. In the timeline, cross-hatching marks restarts and diagonal hatching marks blocked recovery. Operator breaks are also included. FATS allows available robots to keep contributing while affected workers recover or rejoin.

Moving experience between robots and training

Robot actions need timely responses, while transferring a complete attempt or a new policy involves much more data. FATS lets those larger transfers proceed asynchronously alongside interaction and inference. The laboratory network uses two tier-1 and three tier-2 switches, with dual 100 Gbps links between interconnected switches. NAS provides storage at the edge, and GPFS provides shared cloud storage. Experience and policy updates cross the dedicated 30 Gbps edge–cloud link, allowing training to draw on collected attempts while robots keep executing.

06

From collective practice to production

The experiments above show how shared experience can improve execution and how collection can continue through local interruptions. We also applied CEL on Longcheer’s electronics production line, beyond these controlled settings. Eight robots operated there over six days, with a reported operational success rate of 99%. With CEL, the fleet can keep learning after people establish a starting policy through demonstrations and corrections. FERT learns actions from successful attempts and uses failures to choose what to revisit. Our controlled experiments cover five separately trained tasks on one robot platform; outcome feedback and physical resets remain part of the process. More varied tasks and different robot platforms are the next directions to explore.
